# **Optimal Questioning Strategies for Musculoskeletal Pain Assessment in Physical Therapy**

**Introduction: The Foundational Role of Subjective Assessment in Musculoskeletal Pain Management**

The cornerstone of effective physical therapy and rehabilitation for musculoskeletal pain lies in a thorough and detailed assessment process, where the subjective examination serves as the critical initial step.1 This patient interview is not merely a preliminary formality but rather the foundation upon which the entire clinical reasoning process is built.1 A carefully constructed series of questions allows the clinician to gather a comprehensive history of the patient's condition, which is instrumental in formulating diagnostic hypotheses, effectively ruling in or ruling out potential underlying issues, and ultimately establishing a targeted and appropriate plan of care.1 Furthermore, the subjective assessment is the primary avenue for understanding the patient's unique pain experience, encompassing its nuances, triggers, and impact on their daily life and functional abilities.2 This patient-centered approach ensures that the evaluation is tailored to the individual's specific needs and circumstances, moving beyond a generic application of standardized protocols.

Contemporary musculoskeletal pain assessment increasingly embraces the biopsychosocial model, recognizing the intricate interplay of biological, psychological, and social factors in the perception and experience of pain.4 Effective questioning should therefore extend beyond the realm of purely physical symptoms to encompass the psychological landscape of the patient, exploring aspects such as emotional distress, fear-avoidance beliefs, and current coping mechanisms. Simultaneously, social determinants, including work-related factors, family dynamics, and economic considerations, must be investigated to gain a holistic understanding of the individual's situation.4 The identification of red flags, which are indicators of potential serious underlying pathology, alongside other flags such as yellow, orange, blue, and black flags that signify psychosocial and contextual barriers, is a crucial outcome of the subjective assessment, guiding appropriate triage and the formulation of comprehensive intervention strategies.4

The establishment of a strong therapeutic alliance from the very beginning of the interaction is significantly facilitated by the utilization of effective questioning techniques.5 This involves the creation of a comfortable and supportive environment wherein the patient feels genuinely heard and understood. The strategic use of open-ended questions encourages patients to provide detailed narratives and share their experiences in their own words, often revealing critical information that might be overlooked with more direct or leading inquiries.5 Complementing this, the practice of active listening, characterized by behaviors such as maintaining appropriate eye contact, adopting open and receptive body language, and consciously avoiding interruptions, is paramount for building rapport and ensuring the clinician gathers accurate and comprehensive information, fostering a collaborative partnership in the assessment and management of their pain.5

**Standard Musculoskeletal Pain Assessment Frameworks in Physical Therapy**

Physical therapists commonly employ structured frameworks to guide their assessment of musculoskeletal pain, ensuring a systematic and thorough evaluation.1 A typical assessment often begins with a detailed history, obtained through initial questioning that explores the patient's present condition, the history of their present condition, their past medical history, medication history, and relevant social history.1 Following this, a general observation of the patient is conducted, noting their posture, gait, and ability to perform basic movements like sitting and standing, which can provide initial insights into their functional capabilities and limitations.1 The assessment then progresses to a more specific examination of movements, focusing on the active and passive range of motion available at affected joints, as well as an evaluation of muscle strength in various planes and positions.1 Palpation of bones, joints, muscles, and surrounding tissues is another key component, allowing the clinician to identify areas of tenderness, swelling, changes in tissue texture, or other physical abnormalities.1 To further evaluate specific structures or to provoke particular symptoms that aid in differential diagnosis, clinicians utilize a variety of special tests.1 Finally, the integration of standardized tests and outcome measures into the assessment process helps to quantify the patient's functional limitations, establish a baseline, and track their progress in response to interventions over time.1

Another influential framework in musculoskeletal pain management is the "Assess, Predict, Treat" model.6 This approach emphasizes the importance of not only thoroughly assessing the patient's current condition but also utilizing evidence-based decision tools to predict the likely natural and clinical course of common musculoskeletal conditions, particularly those affecting the neck and lower back.6 By focusing on prediction, this framework encourages clinicians to consider the prognosis and to tailor their interventions based on the anticipated trajectory of the patient's pain and functional limitations.6 Ultimately, the "Assess, Predict, Treat" model aims to synthesize the gathered assessment data and prognostic insights into a logical and integrated treatment approach that is highly individualized, taking into account the unique experiences and preferences of each patient alongside the clinician's expertise.6

Regardless of the specific framework that guides the assessment, a systematic and organized approach is paramount for ensuring both thoroughness and accuracy in the evaluation of musculoskeletal pain.1 This includes careful attention to patient preparation, ensuring a comfortable and private environment for the interview and physical examination, as well as a logical and sequential flow of the examination process.1 Comprehensive and accurate documentation of all findings is also an essential element of a robust assessment. To aid in obtaining a complete and relevant history, clinicians frequently employ mnemonics such as LMNOPQRST.1 This mnemonic serves as a structured guide, prompting the clinician to inquire about the **L**ocation of symptoms, the **M**echanism of injury, the **N**ature of the pain, the **O**nset of the problem, **P**alliating and **P**rovoking factors, the **Q**uality of the symptoms, the **R**adiation of the pain, the **S**everity of the pain, and the **T**iming of the symptoms.1 By systematically addressing each of these components, clinicians can ensure a comprehensive understanding of the patient's pain experience.

**Region-Specific Questioning Strategies for Musculoskeletal Pain**

A comprehensive musculoskeletal pain assessment necessitates the use of region-specific questioning strategies tailored to the unique characteristics and potential pathologies associated with different areas of the body.

**Shoulder Pain: Key Diagnostic Questions**

When assessing shoulder pain, several key questions can help to efficiently identify the likely underlying issues.5 Understanding the **onset of the problem** is crucial: "How and when did your shoulder problem start?".5 Determining if the pain began suddenly or gradually and whether it was associated with a specific incident or activity provides valuable diagnostic information.1 Similarly, inquiring about the **location and radiation of pain** with questions like "Where in your shoulder do you feel the most pain?" 9 and "Does the pain radiate down your arm?" 10 can point towards specific anatomical structures involved.5 Exploring the **nature and quality of pain** using questions such as "How would you describe your pain?" 8 (e.g., aching, sharp, burning) can further refine the diagnostic possibilities, as different pain qualities may suggest different underlying tissues or mechanisms.1 Identifying **aggravating and easing factors** through questions like "What makes your shoulder feel better? Worse?" 5 and "Is there pain during specific activity?" 9 is essential for understanding the behavior of the symptoms and narrowing down potential causes.1 Assessing **functional impairments** with questions like "Is it difficult to move your arm?" 10 and "What activities make pain worse?" 8 provides insight into the impact of the pain on the patient's daily life and the severity of the condition.1 Inquiring about a **history of instability** with questions like "Does your shoulder feel unstable?" 9 and "Does your shoulder dislocate or come out of place?" 9 can help identify potential labral tears or capsular laxity.5 Finally, exploring **associated symptoms** such as "Do you have numbness, tingling, or burning in your shoulder or down your arm?" 9 and "Do you hear or feel unusual sensations such as catching, locking, or grinding?" 9 can further aid in differentiating between various shoulder pathologies.5
